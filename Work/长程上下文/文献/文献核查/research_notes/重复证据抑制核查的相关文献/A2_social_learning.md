# A2 — Redundant or correlated information, overconfidence, and information acquisition in economics and psychology (persuasion bias, correlation neglect, costly-information herding, self-confirming / Berk–Nash beliefs, illusory truth)

Novelty check for Paper 1: duplicated same-source evidence in an LLM agent's shared memory makes it overconfident, so it verifies less, and errors persist and spread. Searched 2026-10-21. Each item states how far it was checked. "Abstract" means checked against an abstract or a publisher or repository summary. "Secondary" means only a secondary summary was seen. "Unverified" means not checked. Overlap ratings (High / Medium / Low) are my own judgments, not claims made by the sources. Chain links used below: **L1** = duplicated or correlated evidence → overconfident or too-extreme beliefs; **L2** = overconfidence → less information acquisition or verification; **L3** = less verification → errors that persist or spread.

## 1. Persuasion bias and correlation neglect: what they show about double counting and overconfidence, and whether any show reduced information acquisition

### Takeaway
Theory and incentivized lab experiments both establish that people double-count repeated or overlapping information, which makes their beliefs too extreme and too confident (L1). None of the canonical papers (DeMarzo–Vayanos–Zwiebel 2003, Enke–Zimmermann 2019, Ortoleva–Snowberg 2015, Levy–Razin 2015) models or measures information acquisition: the signals are always given to subjects. The only direct test of acquisition I found is a 2024 dissertation chapter (Auffray). It points the **opposite** way from Paper 1 for redundant information: people who neglect correlation pay *more* for extra correlated signals and are more overprecise.

### Cited Findings

**Theory**
- **DeMarzo, Vayanos & Zwiebel (2003)**, "Persuasion Bias, Social Influence, and Unidimensional Opinions," *QJE* 118(3): 909–968 (the DOI 10.1162/00335530360698469 is from memory and was not checked). Method: a boundedly rational model of repeated communication on a network, in which agents do not adjust for information repeated to them ("persuasion bias"). Finding: an agent's influence on group opinion depends on how central they are in the network, not only on how accurate they are, and opinions on many issues collapse onto one left–right dimension. The abstract contains no information-acquisition or verification choice. Checked: abstract. Overlap: **Medium**. The double-counting mechanism is the same, but the setting is a network of many people, the work is theory only, and there is no confidence or verification outcome. — [LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/454); [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/persuasion-bias-social-influence-unidimensional-opinions)
- **Page (2020)**, *JEBO* 174: 469–477. Tests the unidimensionality implication of persuasion bias with French pre-election survey data and finds political opinions far from unidimensional. Overlap: **Low**, because it tests a downstream implication rather than double counting itself. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jeborg/v174y2020icp469-477.html)
- **Eyster & Rabin (2010)**, "Naïve Herding in Rich-Information Settings," *AEJ: Micro* 2(4): 221–243, DOI 10.1257/mic.2.4.221. Method: a sequential social-learning model in which naive agents believe each earlier action reflects only that person's private signal. Finding: naive agents overweight early movers' signals because later herders' actions carry those same signals again (double counting). They herd on wrong actions with positive probability even in very information-rich settings where rational agents never do. They become fully confident even when wrong, and on average observing others can make them worse off. There is no acquisition choice. Checked: abstract. Overlap: **Medium**. "Copies treated as independent → confident and wrong" is the same logic, but the copies are other people's actions and the work is theory only. — [LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/41889)
- **Eyster & Rabin (2014)**, "Extensive Imitation is Irrational and Harmful," *QJE* 129(4): 1861–1898, DOI 10.1093/qje/qju021. Finding: in most observation structures other than single-file, full rationality requires agents to anti-imitate some of the people they observe. Any learning rule that imitates more than one person without anti-imitating gives positive probability of converging to confident and wrong long-run beliefs. The authors suggest testing whether people follow others *too much*. Checked: abstract. Overlap: **Medium**. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v129y2014i4p1861-1898.html); [Rabin page](https://rabin.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/extensive-imitation-irrational-and-harmful)
- **Ortoleva & Snowberg (2015)**, "Overconfidence in Political Behavior," *AER* 105(2): 504–535 (the DOI 10.1257/aer.20130921 is from memory). Method: a model plus a survey of more than 3,000 U.S. adults. Finding: overconfidence predicts ideological extremeness, voter turnout and strength of partisan identification. The working-paper drafts derive overconfidence from correlational neglect: citizens treat their many experiences as independent signals, although all of them happen to the same person and are correlated. I did not confirm whether the published abstract still uses the term. There is no acquisition outcome. Checked: abstract and working paper. Overlap: **Medium**. It states the "correlation neglect → overconfidence" link explicitly, but the evidence is survey correlations about politics and there is no verification measure. — [Caltech Authors](https://resolver.caltech.edu/CaltechAUTHORS:20150313-134020621); [NBER w19250](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19250.pdf); [2012 draft](https://econen.sufe.edu.cn/_upload/article/files/35/ff/5425c2a34c78ac2c20b1a3094d0b/7b6d13d8-08dc-415c-bc67-a2808406484f.pdf)
- **Levy & Razin (2015a)**, "Correlation Neglect, Voting Behavior, and Information Aggregation," *AER* 105(4): 1634–1645, DOI 10.1257/aer.20140134. Theory: voters underestimate how correlated their information sources are. The resulting extreme beliefs lead some voters to vote on their information rather than their preferences. Under some conditions on the distribution of preferences, this raises vote shares for the best policies and improves information aggregation. Overlap: **Low**. It shows a possible *benefit* of neglect and has no acquisition choice. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v105y2015i4p1634-45.html); [LSE](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/60517)
- **Levy & Razin (2015b)**, "Does Polarisation of Opinions Lead to Polarisation of Platforms? The Case of Correlation Neglect," *Quarterly Journal of Political Science*. When elections are not too competitive, voters who neglect correlation may produce *less* platform polarization than rational voters. Overlap: **Low**. — [LSE](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/62218)
- **Levy, Moreno de Barreda & Razin (2022)**, "Persuasion with Correlation Neglect: A Full Manipulation Result," *AER: Insights* 4(1): 123–138, DOI 10.1257/aeri.20210007. Theory: a sender with unlimited signals can make a correlation-neglecting receiver hold any state-dependent belief the sender chooses. To induce a belief that does not depend on the state, fully correlated (duplicate) signals are enough; other targets need more elaborate correlation structures; a finite number of signals limits the manipulation. Overlap: **Medium**. It shows that mere duplicates of one signal can move a neglecter's belief arbitrarily. The difference is that here a strategic sender creates the duplicates, whereas in Paper 1 they are an architecture artifact; there is no acquisition choice. — [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aeri.20210007); [LSE](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/111551)

**Experiments on double counting**
- **Enke & Zimmermann (2019)**, "Correlation Neglect in Belief Formation," *REStud* 86(1): 313–332, DOI 10.1093/restud/rdx081. Method: incentivized lab experiments in which subjects receive overlapping (correlated) signals, so that rational updating requires undoing the double counting. Finding: many subjects ignore the overlap. Their beliefs react too strongly when the same story is retold and swing too much. The bias varies a lot and systematically across subjects, and the paper studies its mechanisms. The 2013 working paper adds an experimental asset market in which correlation neglect drives over-optimism and over-pessimism and affects aggregate outcomes. Signals are given, not chosen; there is no acquisition or verification choice. Checked: abstract. Overlap: **Medium-high** for L1, since duplication is manipulated causally in humans. Differences: human subjects, abstract signals, no verification outcome, no provenance labels. — [Enke PDF](https://benjamin-enke.com/pdf/Correlation_neglect.pdf); [IDEAS (WP)](https://ideas.repec.org/p/zbw/bonedp/042013.html)
- **Brandts, Giritligil & Weber (2015)**, "An Experimental Study of Persuasion Bias and Social Influence in Networks," *European Economic Review* (volume and pages not checked). Method: four-person networks whose structure everyone can see; private signals about an unknown parameter; estimates are shared repeatedly. Finding: agents with more outgoing links get more weight than optimal updating would give them, consistent with persuasion bias. The results contradict a rival heuristic in which agents with more *incoming* links are most influential. Checked: working-paper abstract. Overlap: **Low-medium**. — [BSE working paper](https://bse.eu/research/working-papers/experimental-study-persuasion-bias-and-social-influence-networks); [Bilgi repository](https://openaccess.bilgi.edu.tr/items/ad568902-4a4f-4d1e-ae02-cc250cf24215/full)
- **Corazzini, Pavesi, Petrovich & Stanca (2012)**, "Influential Listeners: An Experiment on Persuasion Bias in Social Networks" (I believe it is in *European Economic Review*; not checked). According to a search-engine summary of its abstract (page not opened), the most influential agents had more *incoming* links, contrary to persuasion bias, and an updating rule using both in-degree and out-degree fit better. Checked: secondary only. Overlap: **Low-medium**; the evidence on persuasion bias is mixed. The rival in-degree heuristic is described in — [Brandts et al. abstract](https://bse.eu/research/working-papers/experimental-study-persuasion-bias-and-social-influence-networks)
- **Eyster, Rabin & Weizsäcker (2018, working paper)**, "An Experiment on Social Mislearning," CRC TRR 190 Discussion Paper 73 and CEPR DP11020. Method: subjects estimate the sum of all earlier signals plus their own after seeing predecessors' estimates. Finding: in the single-file treatment about 75% act rationally (they add their own signal to the immediate predecessor's estimate), while the other 25% neglect redundancy and imitate too much. In a four-per-period treatment where equilibrium requires anti-imitation, anti-imitation appears in 16% of cases overall (35% in the most transparent cases). Redundancy neglect makes late estimates far too extreme, and subjects would have earned more on average by ignoring predecessors altogether. I did not check whether it has been published since. Overlap: **Medium**. — [Rabin abstract page](https://rabin.scholars.harvard.edu/abstract-experiment-social-mislearning); [LMU ePub DP 73](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58086/1/73.pdf); [CEPR](https://cepr.org/publications/dp11020)
- **Angrisani, Guarino, Jehiel & Kitagawa (2021)**, "Information Redundancy Neglect versus Overconfidence: A Social Learning Experiment," *AEJ: Micro* 13(3): 163–197, DOI 10.1257/mic.20180394. Method: each subject reports a belief after seeing predecessors' reports and a private signal. Finding: the data fit an overconfidence model (people overweight their own signal) and contradict both perfect Bayesian equilibrium and the redundancy-neglect model. Overlap: **Medium, as counter-evidence**: in a transparent sequence, people did not double count. — [HAL](https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-03325779)
- **Chandrasekhar, Larreguy & Xandri (2020)**, "Testing Models of Social Learning on Networks: Evidence from Two Experiments," *Econometrica* 88(1): 1–32, DOI 10.3982/ECTA14407. Method: lab-in-the-field experiments with 665 villagers in India and 350 students at ITAM in Mexico, fitting a structural mix of Bayesian agents and DeGroot agents (who average their neighbors' guesses). Finding: the estimated share of Bayesian agents is about 10% among villagers and about 50% among students. Eyster–Rabin–Weizsäcker cite this as field evidence that agents fail to account for redundancy. Overlap: **Low-medium**. — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2020/01/01/testing-models-social-learning-networks-evidence-two); [ERW page](https://rabin.scholars.harvard.edu/abstract-experiment-social-mislearning)
- **Mobius, Phan & Szeidl (2015)**, "Treasure Hunt: Social Learning in the Field," NBER WP 21014 / CEPR DP10493. Method: noisy information seeded into a real social network. Finding: information travels only about two steps through conversations. Once imperfect spread is accounted for, the data fit best a "tagged" model, in which people track where information came from and so avoid double counting. Overlap: **Medium, as counter-evidence and as a design reference**: source tagging is the human mechanism that Paper 1's provenance labels manipulate. — [CEPR DP10493](https://cepr.org/publications/DP10493); [summary](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/w21014.html)
- **Bolte & Fan (2024)**, "Motivated Mislearning: The Case of Correlation Neglect," *JEBO* 217: 647–663, DOI 10.1016/j.jebo.2023.11.020. Method: possibly redundant signals about the subject's own IQ-test performance, or about a state that does not affect the subject's ego. Finding: subjects underestimate how likely identical signals are to come from the same source, and more so for flattering signals. The asymmetry disappears when the state does not concern the subject's ego. Overlap: **Low-medium**. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jeborg/v217y2024icp647-663.html)

**Demand for correlated information (the only acquisition evidence found)**
- **Auffray (2024)**, *Essays in Behavioral and Experimental Economics*, PhD dissertation, Washington University in St. Louis (chair: Brian Rogers), chapter 2: "Correlation neglect and overconfidence: theory and experimental evidence." Method: an experiment in which subjects acquire highly correlated signals themselves, with two incentivized measures of correlation neglect. Findings: people who neglect correlation value and seek additional information *more*. Full neglecters pay more for additional information, and high neglecters show more overprecision. The author suggests that drawing attention to correlation may reduce overconfidence. I found no peer-reviewed version. Overlap: **High on L1 and L2, but with the opposite sign on acquisition**. In this design the extra information is another *correlated* signal, not an independent check; subjects are human. — [WashU repository](https://repository.library.washu.edu/art_sci_etds/3003)

**Classic psychology: redundancy and confidence**
- **Davis, Lohse & Kottemann (1994)**, "Harmful Effects of Seemingly Helpful Information on Forecasts of Stock Earnings," *Journal of Economic Psychology* 15(2): 253–267. Method: MBA students forecast stock earnings. Finding: redundant information made subjects significantly more confident than at baseline, while forecast accuracy fell in both the redundant and the non-redundant conditions. - **Tversky & Kahneman (1974)**, "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," *Science* 185(4157): 1124–1131, DOI 10.1126/science.185.4157.1124. Overlap: **Medium**; this is the classic statement of L1. — [Wikipedia: Illusion of validity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_validity); [Cambridge chapter page](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/judgment-under-uncertainty/on-the-psychology-of-prediction/8B2E60F150DA1BBA2BFEB5B3FCDA74C6)

According to the abstract, people often fail to value uncorrelated (non-redundant) sources, and whether they prefer redundancy depends on their intuitive theory of information. Checked: abstract only; design not seen. Overlap: **Medium**. ### Inferences
- L1 (double counting → beliefs too extreme or too confident) is well established, both in theory (DeMarzo et al., Eyster–Rabin, Levy et al.) and in causal lab manipulations of duplicated signals (Enke–Zimmermann; Eyster–Rabin–Weizsäcker; Davis et al.). Paper 1 cannot present L1 as new. It should cite this work and frame its own contribution as testing the phenomenon in LLM agents whose memory makes the copies.
- Human evidence is not uniform. In transparent sequences people overweight their own signals rather than neglecting redundancy (Angrisani et al.). Field data fit source tagging (Mobius et al.). In the single-file treatment, about 75% of subjects were rational (Eyster–Rabin–Weizsäcker). How much people neglect duplication depends on how visible the dependence is, which is exactly what Paper 1's provenance labels vary.
- On acquisition, no canonical correlation-neglect paper shows reduced acquisition or verification. Auffray (2024) finds the opposite for redundant signals. Paper 1 should therefore separate two behaviors and pre-register the expected sign of each: "consult memory again or acquire another copy," which neglect may *increase*, and "independent re-observation," which Paper 1 predicts neglect *decreases*.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full texts of Enke–Zimmermann (mechanism treatments), Ortoleva–Snowberg (how overconfidence was measured) or Auffray (design, effect sizes, and whether neglecters also make fewer independent checks).
- Volume and pages for Brandts et al. and Corazzini et al. are unverified; Corazzini et al. was seen only through a secondary summary.
- A search snippet mentioned a Kansai University RISS discussion paper (No. 69) reporting that subjects overvalue moderately or strongly correlated signals and undervalue weakly correlated ones. I could not find or verify it (snippet URL: https://www.kansai-u.ac.jp/riss/output/paper/pdf/RISS_DP_No69.pdf).
- A Tel Aviv University study (N = 481) on decision-support systems reportedly found that people buy information more often when the aid is *not* correlated with what they already know, i.e. partial sensitivity to redundancy. Authors and venue were not verified: https://cris.tau.ac.il/en/publications/normative-or-confirmative-effects-of-information-quality-and-redu/

## 2. Herding and cascades with costly private information: is it established that observing others' actions reduces costly information acquisition?

### Takeaway
For rational agents in theory, **yes**. When private information is costly, agents free-ride on the public history: they buy a signal only if it could change the action the public history points to, so acquisition, and with it learning, stops once public beliefs are strong enough. Learning is complete only if some agents can get informative signals at arbitrarily low cost (Burguet–Vives 2000; Mueller-Frank–Pai 2016; Ali 2018; the 2024 JEL survey). Lab evidence is mixed. Subjects often choose social over private information (Duffy et al. 2019; Goeree–Yariv 2015), yet they buy *too many* costly signals relative to equilibrium (Kübler–Weizsäcker 2004) and follow others too little (Weizsäcker 2010). I found no model that combines redundancy or correlation neglect with costly information acquisition.

### Cited Findings

**Rational theory of costly acquisition**
- **Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch (1992)**, "A Theory of Fads, Fashion, Custom, and Cultural Change as Informational Cascades," *JPE* 100(5): 992–1026 (the DOI 10.1086/261849 is from memory). A cascade occurs when it is optimal for an individual to follow predecessors whatever their own signal says; the model explains local conformity and the fragility of mass behavior. **I could not check whether BHW analyze costly signals**: the available PDF is a scanned image. Overlap: **Low-medium**. — [Stanford SNAP copy](https://snap.stanford.edu/class/cs224w-readings/bikhchandani92fads.pdf); [summary](https://stafforini.com/works/bikhchandani-1992-theory-fads-fashion/)
- **Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, Tamuz & Welch (2024)**, "Information Cascades and Social Learning," *Journal of Economic Literature* 62(3): 1040–1093, DOI 10.1257/jel.20241472 (survey). Its points, paraphrased:
  - Even small acquisition costs make agents eventually stop acquiring private signals. Aggregation is then fully blocked, even when signals are unbounded (§4.3.6, §7).
  - Asymptotic learning does not survive the introduction of costs for acquiring or processing private information (summarizing Burguet–Vives, Mueller-Frank–Pai and Ali).
  - Information externalities can lead agents to acquire too little. When acquisition is observable, there is a strategic incentive to acquire less (§5.1).
  - Only a few agents acquire information, a version of Galeotti and Goyal's (2010) "Law of the Few."
  - When investigation is costly and observing others is noisy, free-riding can make social observation *lower* decision quality (the groupthink discussion, §7.3).
  - Cascades form quickly even when private signals are very noisy (§2.2).
  - Persuasion bias and correlation neglect are described as people treating information that came from others as independent.

  Overlap: **Medium**; a consolidated statement of the rational mechanism. — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.11044); [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jel.20241472)
- **Burguet & Vives (2000)**, "Social Learning and Costly Information Acquisition," *Economic Theory* 15(1): 185–205, DOI 10.1007/s001990050006. Theory: short-lived agents decide how much costly private precision to buy, given a noisy public average of past predictions. Costly acquisition stops public information from accumulating without bound if and only if the marginal cost of information is positive at zero. Overlap: **Medium**. It shows more public information → less private acquisition → learning stalls, but with rational agents, continuous actions and a social setting. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/joecth/v15y2000i1p185-205.html); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s001990050006)
- **Ali (2018)**, "Herding with Costly Information," *JET* 175: 713–729, DOI 10.1016/j.jet.2018.02.009. Theory: players avoid the cost and free-ride on the public history, and a player buys information only if it could change her action. With a coarse action space, learning is complete if and only if, for every cost c > 0, a positive share of agent types can buy, for less than c, an experiment able to overturn the public history. The results are applied to market efficiency. Overlap: **Medium-high on mechanism**: a strong current belief, which in Paper 1 is inflated by copies, raises the bar for verification to be worth its cost. Differences: agents are rational, belief strength is correct rather than inflated, and the setting is social. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jetheo/v175y2018icp713-729.html); [PSU PDF](https://sites.psu.edu/nageebali/files/2023/07/AliHerdingCostlyInfoJET.pdf)
- **Mueller-Frank & Pai (2016)**, "Social Learning with Costly Search," *AEJ: Micro* 8(1): 83–109, DOI 10.1257/mic.20130253. Theory: agents with private, independently drawn search costs search over actions in turn after observing predecessors. Asymptotic learning holds only if search costs are not bounded away from zero. With two actions, the probability that late movers choose wrong falls at a linear rate. Overlap: **Medium**. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aejmic/v8y2016i1p83-109.html)
- **Hendricks, Sorensen & Wiseman (2012)**: with heterogeneous preferences and costly private information, learning can be incomplete. **Song (2016)**: when observing predecessors is costly, early agents can end up sacrificing themselves to inform later ones. **Kultti & Miettinen**: herding models with costly information and costly observation. All three were checked only through the JEL survey. Overlap: **Low-medium**. — [JEL survey §7 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.11044)
- **Liang & Mu (2020)**, "Complementary Information and Learning Traps," *QJE* 135(1): 389–448, DOI 10.1093/qje/qjz033. Theory: short-lived agents choose among flexibly correlated information sources, and what they learn is passed on. In the long run, either information is aggregated efficiently or the community falls into a "learning trap" and keeps sampling suboptimal sources. Which happens depends on how the sources complement each other: the size of the smallest complementary set compared with the number of unknowns. Overlap: **Medium**. The correlation structure of sources shapes what agents acquire and can lock in inefficiency; differences: rational agents, social setting, no double counting. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v135y2020i1p389-448..html); [Penn WP](https://economics.sas.upenn.edu/system/files/working-papers/17-024%20comp-info%20%28002%29.pdf)
- **Bolton & Harris (1999)**, "Strategic Experimentation," *Econometrica* 67(2): 349–374. Theory: a two-armed bandit played by many agents, with public information. It has a free-rider effect (let others experiment) and an encouragement effect (others' future experimentation makes experimenting now more attractive). In the later exponential-bandit model of Keller, Rady & Cripps, free-riding is extreme and there is no encouragement effect. Overlap: **Low-medium**. — [Econometric Society](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1999/03/01/strategic-experimentation); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecm/emetrp/v67y1999i2p349-374.html)

**Naive or misspecified herding (correlated actions treated as independent)**
- **Bohren (2016)**, "Informational Herding with Model Misspecification," *JET* 163: 222–247, DOI 10.1016/j.jet.2016.01.011. Theory: some agents act only on their private signal while others also observe history. Agents who overestimate how much new information past actions carry, that is, who treat herders as independent, can have their beliefs entrenched on the wrong state even though public information would eventually reveal the truth. The JEL survey adds that *under*-estimation does not stop convergence, because agents acting on private signals break up wrong cascades. Overlap: **Medium-high conceptually** ("treating copies as independent sources → entrenched wrong belief"). Differences: social setting, theory only, no costly verification. — [IDEAS (WP)](https://ideas.repec.org/p/pen/papers/14-007.html); [published PDF](https://economics.northwestern.edu/docs/events/nemmers/2018/bohren2.pdf); [JEL survey](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.11044)
- **Frick, Iijima & Ishii (2020)**, "Misinterpreting Others and the Fragility of Social Learning," *Econometrica* 88(6): 2281–2328, DOI 10.3982/ECTA16981. Small misperceptions of the distribution of others' types can lead agents to put probability one on a fixed state whatever the truth. How fragile learning is depends on how rich the payoff-relevant uncertainty is. Overlap: **Low-medium**. — [Econometric Society](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2020/11/01/misinterpreting-others-and-fragility-social-learning)
- **Dasaratha & He (2020)**, "Network Structure and Naive Sequential Learning," *Theoretical Economics*. Checked: abstract level, via a search summary. Overlap: **Low-medium**. — [TE page](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/theoretical-economics/2020/05/01/Network-structure-and-naive-sequential-learning); [experiment, arXiv 1909.02220](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.02220)
- **Gagnon-Bartsch & Rabin (2016, working paper)**, "Naive Social Learning, Mislearning, and Unlearning." Checked: secondary only. — [Rabin page PDF](https://rabin.scholars.harvard.edu/resource/gagnon-bartschrabin2016pdf)

**Lab evidence with costly signals or a choice of information**
- **Kübler & Weizsäcker (2004)**, "Limited Depth of Reasoning and Failure of Cascade Formation in the Laboratory," *REStud* 71(2): 425–441. Method: a cascade game in which each player decides whether to buy a private signal at a small positive cost. In equilibrium only the first player buys, and everyone after herds without buying. Finding: subjects buy too many signals and the equilibrium prediction performs poorly. An error-rate model shows that inferences get noisier at higher levels of reasoning and that subjects use only very short chains of reasoning. Overlap: **Medium, as counter-evidence**: people stop acquiring later than rational theory predicts. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v71y2004i2p425-441.html); [HU Berlin edoc](https://edoc.hu-berlin.de/18452/4096)
- **Weizsäcker (2010)**, "Do We Follow Others When We Should? A Simple Test of Rational Expectations," *AER* (2010; volume and pages not checked). Method: pooled data from 13 cascade experiments. Finding: subjects abandon their own signal only when the odds that it is wrong exceed about 2:1, not 1:1 as rationality requires. Where following others was the best choice, they captured only about 53% of the attainable payoff. Overlap: **Low-medium**, as counter-evidence that people underuse social information. — [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/14349/1/14349.pdf); [LSE](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/4945)
- **Duffy, Hopkins, Kornienko & Ma (2019)**, "Information Choice in a Social Learning Experiment," *GEB* 118: 295–315, DOI 10.1016/j.geb.2019.06.008. Method: a variant of the Anderson–Holt design in which subjects choose *either* a private signal *or* social information (predecessors' guesses). Finding: most subjects choose social information more often than is optimal; a substantial minority follow a refined equilibrium, and some always pick the same source. Overlap: **Medium**: in humans, social information crowds out acquiring private information. Differences: no duplication manipulation, and the choice is binary. — [Southampton ePrints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/479183); [PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~duffy/papers/GEB19.pdf)
- **Goeree & Yariv (2015)**, "Conformity in the Lab," *Journal of the Economic Science Association* 1(1): 15–28 (sources disagree on the last page), DOI 10.1007/s40881-015-0001-7. Method: subjects choose between an informative private signal and a social signal that carries no information (the choices of predecessors who skipped the private signal). Finding: the social signal was chosen 34% of the time, and 88% of those who chose it followed predecessors. Under majority rule it was chosen 51% of the time, and still about half the time with five-fold stakes. Overlap: **Medium**: people replace their own information gathering with others' choices even when those choices carry no information. — [Caltech Authors](https://resolver.caltech.edu/CaltechAUTHORS:20170616-104931170); [Cambridge](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-economic-science-association/article/abs/conformity-in-the-lab/15DD8A42A828C9507E717AF181FD8ED0)

### Inferences
- Paper 1 can borrow Ali's (2018) principle as its rational benchmark: verification is worth paying for only if it could change the action. If duplicated memory entries inflate the planner's belief, the highest cost at which verifying is still worthwhile should fall as copies increase. The curve of "probability of verifying" against verification cost should then shift down or left with copy count. If the agent uses same-source labels correctly, labeled copies should produce no shift. This gives a sharp, pre-registrable prediction for the cost sweep.
- Existing theory delivers "public information crowds out private acquisition" when public beliefs are *correct*. Naive-herding theory delivers "duplicated information → confident wrong beliefs" with no acquisition choice. The combination, naive double counting plus costly verification → earlier stopping → persistent error, was not formalized in anything I found. That is a plausible gap, but my search for it was short.
- Lab evidence shows people both over-acquire (Kübler–Weizsäcker) and under-use social information (Weizsäcker 2010). LLM planners could deviate from the rational benchmark in either direction, so Paper 1 needs a baseline of verification behavior with no duplication at every cost level.

### Gaps
- BHW's (1992) treatment of costly signals is unverified (scanned PDF). Banerjee (1992), Grossman & Stiglitz (1980), Kultti & Miettinen, Hendricks et al. and Song (2016) were not checked against their own abstracts.
- I found no model combining redundancy or correlation neglect with costly acquisition, after one targeted search. Before claiming this gap in the paper, a fuller search is needed, e.g. Google Scholar papers citing both Ali (2018) and Eyster–Rabin (2010).
- I found no lab study that manipulates duplication of social information and measures how many costly private signals subjects buy.

## 3. Self-confirming and Berk–Nash equilibrium: how they formalize wrong beliefs that persist because the agent stops collecting disconfirming evidence

### Takeaway
Self-confirming equilibrium (Fudenberg–Levine 1993) allows beliefs to be wrong wherever the agent's own actions never produce data: off-path beliefs are never tested, so they are never contradicted. Berk–Nash equilibrium (Esponda–Pouzo 2016) adds misspecified models: beliefs are the best fit, in the Kullback–Leibler (KL) sense, to the data the agent's own strategy generates, so a wrong model combined with self-generated data can be a stable steady state. Follow-up work shows that:
- overconfidence can make learning self-defeating (Heidhues–Kőszegi–Strack 2018);
- patience and willingness to experiment change long-run beliefs (Fudenberg–Romanyuk–Strack 2017);
- lab subjects sustain self-confirming-type errors and wrong-mental-model errors despite feedback (Fudenberg–Vespa 2019; Esponda–Vespa–Yuksel 2024).

### Cited Findings
- **Fudenberg & Levine (1993)**, "Self-Confirming Equilibrium," *Econometrica* 61(3): 523–545, DOI 10.2307/2951716. Each player best-responds to beliefs that need be correct only along the equilibrium path. If the equilibrium is played repeatedly, no player ever sees play that contradicts their beliefs, even though beliefs about off-path play may be wrong. The paper characterizes how such equilibria can differ from Nash and gives conditions under which they coincide with standard solution concepts. Overlap: **Medium**. It is a formal template for "the error persists because the action that would reveal it (re-observing) is never taken." Differences: games, an equilibrium concept, no duplication or confidence. — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1993/05/01/self-confirming-equilibrium); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecm/emetrp/v61y1993i3p523-45.html)
- **Esponda & Pouzo (2016)**, "Berk–Nash Equilibrium: A Framework for Modeling Agents with Misspecified Models," *Econometrica* 84(3): 1093–1130, DOI 10.3982/ECTA12609. Each player has a subjective model, possibly misspecified, that defines which beliefs about consequences are feasible. Equilibrium requires that the strategy be optimal given the belief, and that the belief be the best fit (minimum KL divergence) among those feasible beliefs. Because the data depend on the strategy, the KL divergence does too. Nash and self-confirming equilibrium are special cases with correctly specified models. The paper also gives a learning foundation. A follow-up, "Equilibrium in Misspecified Markov Decision Processes," notes that its static case coincides with single-agent Berk–Nash (I recall it appeared in *Theoretical Economics* in 2021; not checked). Overlap: **Medium-high conceptually**. An agent that treats duplicated memory entries as conditionally independent has a misspecified likelihood, and Berk–Nash describes its steady state given its own verification policy. Differences: theory only, no LLMs, no causal test. — [Econometric Society](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2016/05/01/berk–nash-equilibrium-framework-modeling-agents-misspecified); [arXiv 1411.1152](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1411.1152); [MDP follow-up, arXiv 1502.06901](https://arxiv.org/html/1502.06901)
- **Fudenberg, Romanyuk & Strack (2017)**, "Active Learning with a Misspecified Prior," *Theoretical Economics* 12(3): 1155–1189, DOI 10.3982/TE2480. A Bayesian agent whose prior rules out the true state chooses actions and sees noisy payoffs. The paper fully characterizes long-run actions and beliefs when the agent considers two possible states. In a three-action example, a myopic agent's beliefs converge but a sufficiently patient agent's do not, an interaction between misspecification and discounting. Overlap: **Medium**: how much the agent chooses to experiment interacts with misspecification. — [Econometric Society TE](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/theoretical-economics/2017/09/26/Active-learning-with-a-misspecified-prior)
- **Heidhues, Kőszegi & Strack (2018)**, "Unrealistic Expectations and Misguided Learning," *Econometrica* 86(4): 1159–1214, DOI 10.3982/ECTA14084. An overconfident agent learns about an external fundamental. Beliefs converge under weak conditions, but in a broad class of settings learning is self-defeating. Overconfidence makes the agent too pessimistic about the fundamental; the worse outcomes that follow feed more pessimism, and he drifts away from the truth even if he started out correct. An underconfident agent's mistaken learning limits itself. Overlap: **Medium**: overconfidence plus self-generated data gives a persistent, compounding error. Differences: the overconfidence concerns the agent's own ability, not duplicated evidence, and there is no verification choice. — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2018/07/01/unrealistic-expectations-and-misguided-learning); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/emetrp/v86y2018i4p1159-1214.html)
- **Fudenberg & Vespa (2019)**, "Learning Theory and Heterogeneous Play in a Signaling-Game Experiment," *AEJ: Micro* 11(4): 186–215, DOI 10.1257/mic.20180317. In a lab signaling game, a non-Nash self-confirming equilibrium (some senders play "Out" and so never learn how receivers would respond) is predicted to persist when each subject keeps a fixed sender type, but not when types are redrawn every period. The data show more "Out" play in the fixed-type treatment. Overlap: **Medium**: causal lab evidence that not sampling keeps wrong beliefs alive. Differences: human subjects, a strategic game, no duplication. — [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/mic.20180317); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aejmic/v11y2019i4p186-215.html)
- **Esponda, Vespa & Yuksel (2024)**, "Mental Models and Learning: The Case of Base-Rate Neglect," *AER* (volume and pages not checked), DOI 10.1257/aer.20201004. Lab finding: base-rate neglect persists despite plentiful feedback. Errors rooted in wrong mental models are the most persistent, because subjects are confident in their initial answers and engage less with feedback. After 200 rounds, beliefs are further from the Bayesian benchmark than in a control treatment where base-rate neglect is not available as a mental model. Showing subjects a summary of the feedback so far substantially improves beliefs and eliminates base-rate neglect. The paper extends the idea to voting, where subjects fail to account for being pivotal. Overlap: **Medium-high**: "wrong model → confidence → less engagement with disconfirming evidence → persistence," plus a remedy (aggregated feedback) that resembles a provenance-aware memory summary. Differences: the feedback is free, not costly verification, and the error is base-rate neglect, not duplication. — [PSU PDF](https://econ.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2023/05/EspondaVespaYuksel.pdf); [UCSB PDF](https://faculty.econ.ucsb.edu/~sevgi/EspondaVespaYuksel.pdf)
- **Bohren & Hauser**, "Learning with Heterogeneous Misspecified Models" (cited as 2019 in the JEL survey; I recall *Econometrica* 2021, not checked). When incorrect herds last forever, beliefs converge almost surely to the wrong state. Checked only through the survey. — [JEL survey (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.11044)
- **Liang & Mu (2020)** (see §2): persistent sampling of suboptimal sources ("learning traps") as a long-run outcome. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v135y2020i1p389-448..html)

### Inferences
- The literature offers two separate persistence channels, and Paper 1 can use both.
  - Self-confirming channel: the agent's action never produces the disconfirming observation. If the agent never re-observes the knife's location, the false memory is never tested.
  - Berk–Nash channel: even observed data are fitted through a wrong model, here one that treats copies as independent.
  Paper 1's "persistent error" outcome corresponds to the first channel when verification is skipped, and to the second when the agent re-observes but the duplicated memory still outweighs the new observation. Coding these two cases separately would link the results directly to this theory.
- In self-confirming theory, persistence depends on the value of experimenting compared with its cost; Paper 1's verification-cost sweep is the empirical counterpart. Fudenberg–Romanyuk–Strack's patience result suggests the remaining task horizon should matter as well.
- Esponda–Vespa–Yuksel give a near-template for a remedy treatment: if the mechanism is model-based confidence, summarizing evidence with deduplicated sources should reduce persistence.

### Gaps
- Not checked: Fudenberg & Levine's companion paper "Steady State Learning and Nash Equilibrium" (1993), Fudenberg & Kreps (1995), Kalai & Lehrer (1993), and the incomplete-learning results of Rothschild (1974) and Easley & Kiefer (1988).
- Venue details for the Esponda–Pouzo MDP paper and for Bohren–Hauser are unverified.
- Apart from Bohren's herding model, I found no self-confirming or Berk–Nash paper that explicitly models duplicated or correlated evidence as the misspecification.

## 4. The illusory truth (repetition) effect, and work on repetition and confidence or information search in humans

### Takeaway
Repetition reliably raises perceived truth, even against what people know, with a logarithmic dose–response (Hasher et al. 1977; Fazio et al. 2015; Hassan & Barber 2021). Most relevant to Paper 1, people treat one source repeated as if it were several independent sources (Weaver et al. 2007; Yousif et al. 2019), and they correct only partly when the dependence is made explicit (Connor Desai et al. 2022; Mercier & Miton 2019). Experimentally lowering confidence while holding accuracy fixed increases information seeking (Desender et al. 2018), and dogmatism predicts less seeking (Schulz et al. 2020). However, I found **no study showing that repetition itself reduces fact-checking or information search**.

### Cited Findings
- **Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino (1977)**, "Frequency and the Conference of Referential Validity," *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 16: 107–112. Method: 60 plausible statements rated in three sessions two weeks apart, some of them repeated. Finding: validity ratings rose for repeated statements, whether true or false, and stayed flat for new ones. Overlap: **Low-medium**. This is the origin of the repetition → belief finding, but it has no source structure and no search measure. — [Hasher lab abstract](https://hasherlab.psych.utoronto.ca/abstracts/hasher_etal_JVLVB_77.htm)
- **Begg, Anas & Farinacci (1992)**, "Dissociation of Processes in Belief: Source Recollection, Statement Familiarity, and the Illusion of Truth," *JEP: General* 121: 446–458. A two-process account: familiarity raises perceived truth automatically, while remembering the source takes deliberate control. Illusory truth arises when a statement feels familiar but its source or truth label is not recalled. Checked: secondary only. Overlap: **Medium**, and directly relevant to provenance labels: a label helps only if it is retrieved and used. — [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusory_truth_effect)
- **Fazio, Brashier, Payne & Marsh (2015)**, "Knowledge Does Not Protect Against Illusory Truth," *JEP: General* 144(5): 993–1002, DOI 10.1037/xge0000098. Experiment 1: repetition raised perceived truth even for statements that contradicted what participants knew. Experiment 2: multinomial modeling showed participants sometimes relied on fluency even when their knowledge was available ("knowledge neglect"). Overlap: **Medium**; the analog is duplicated memory overriding contrary evidence. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26301795/)
- **Hassan & Barber (2021)**, "The Effects of Repetition Frequency on the Illusory Truth Effect," *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications* 6, DOI 10.1186/s41235-021-00301-5. Method: up to 9 repetitions (Experiment 1) and up to 27 (Experiment 2). Finding: perceived truth rose logarithmically with repetitions, with the biggest gain at the second exposure. Overlap: **Medium-high** for a copy-count dose–response. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8116821/)
- **Weaver, Garcia, Schwarz & Miller (2007)**, "Inferring the Popularity of an Opinion from Its Familiarity: A Repetitive Voice Can Sound Like a Chorus," *JPSP* 92(5): 821–833. Six experiments: people judge a familiar opinion to be widely held even when all the familiarity comes from one person repeating it. The effect persisted when participants knew the opinions came from one person, and it is driven by how easily the opinion comes to mind rather than by deliberate inference. A secondary summary reports that one person saying an opinion three times had about 90% of the effect of three different people saying it once; this figure is unverified. Overlap: **High**. It is the closest human analog of "one misperception copied into several stores looks like several sources." Differences: social opinions and consensus estimates, no verification measure. — [USC PDF](https://dornsife.usc.edu/norbert-schwarz/wp-content/uploads/sites/231/2023/11/07_jpsp_weaver_et_al_chorus.pdf); [Haifa CRIS](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/inferring-the-popularity-of-an-opinion-from-its-familiarity-a-rep/); [secondary source for the 90% figure](https://www.spring.org.uk/?p=5066)
- **Yousif, Aboody & Keil (2019)**, "The Illusion of Consensus: A Failure to Distinguish Between True and False Consensus," *Psychological Science* 30: 1195–1204, DOI 10.1177/0956797619856844. Four preregistered experiments with news-like reports. Participants were equally confident in conclusions from a true consensus (independent primary sources) and from a false consensus (several reports all based on one primary source). This held even immediately after they had stated that a true consensus is more believable. A corrigendum appeared in August 2020; I did not check what it changed. Overlap: **High**. It reproduces the exact structure of Paper 1's manipulation (many secondary reports, one primary source) in humans. Differences: confidence ratings only, no verification or acquisition, no agent. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797619856844); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797619856844/)
- **Connor Desai, Xie & Hayes (2022)**, "Getting to the Source of the Illusion of Consensus," *Cognition* 223: 105023. Telling people how the original sources used evidence to reach their conclusions reduced the illusion. People favored a true consensus when the relationship between sources was unambiguous, but they did not fully discount a false consensus. A 2025 Cognitive Science Society paper by the same group reports that independent consensus beat dependent consensus only when independence was strongly emphasized. Checked: press release and secondary. Overlap: **Medium-high**: the human analog of provenance labels, which correct the illusion only partly. — [UNSW news](https://www.unsw.edu.au/newsroom/news/2022/02/how-can-we-get-better-at-telling-misinformation-from-reliable-ex); [CogSci 2025 PDF (secondary)](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/49240/galley/38746/download)
- **Mercier & Miton (2019)**, "Utilizing Simple Cues to Informational Dependency," *Evolution and Human Behavior* 40(3): 301–314, DOI 10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2019.01.001. People discount a majority whose members got their view from the same hearsay, from witnessing the same event, or from a shared motivation. They do not discount when members merely share cognitive traits, and they do discount when one member is untrustworthy. Overlap: **Medium, as counter-evidence**: people use concrete dependency cues of the kind that were reliable during human evolution. — [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/utilizing-simple-cues-informational-dependency); [x-mol](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/955239)
- **Desender, Boldt & Yeung (2018)**, "Subjective Confidence Predicts Information Seeking in Decision Making," *Psychological Science* 29(5): 761–778, DOI 10.1177/0956797617744771. Method: a perceptual task in which conditions were matched in accuracy while the strength and the variability of the evidence were varied independently. Finding: variable evidence lowered confidence, and participants sought more information when confidence was low, even though accuracy was the same. Original sample N = 20, with a preregistered replication of N = 50. Overlap: **Medium-high** for L2 with accuracy held fixed; the design logic resembles Paper 1's fixed-image manipulation. Differences: human subjects, no duplication. — [UKE portal](https://fis.uke.de/portal/en/publications/subjective-confidence-predicts-information-seeking-in-decision-making(7b82e2b5-2545-4811-813f-7c74a1329a35).html); [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10076911/)
- **Schulz, Rollwage, Dolan & Fleming (2020)**, "Dogmatism Manifests in Lowered Information Search under Uncertainty," *PNAS*, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2009641117. Method: two general-population samples (n = 370 and n = 364). Finding: more dogmatic participants were less likely to seek more information before committing to a perceptual decision, and used their uncertainty less to guide that search. Overlap: **Low-medium**; the evidence is a trait-level correlation. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7733856/)
- **Vellani, Zheng, Ercelik & Sharot (2023)**, "The Illusory Truth Effect Leads to the Spread of Misinformation," *Cognition*, DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2023.105421. Method: two experiments, N = 260. Finding: a single earlier exposure raised willingness to share statements, for both health and general-knowledge items, and the effect ran through higher perceived accuracy. Overlap: **Low-medium**: repetition → belief → passing it on, the analog of errors spreading downstream. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10636596)
- **Brashier, Eliseev & Marsh (2020, *Cognition*)**: asking people to rate truth (rather than interest) at first exposure reduced later illusory truth, but only when they had the relevant knowledge. Checked: secondary only. — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/when-false-claims-are-repeated-we-start-believe-they-are-true)

### Inferences
- **Copy count:** the human work predicts a concave, roughly logarithmic effect of copies on belief (Hassan & Barber) and almost full "chorus" credit for one repeated voice (Weaver et al.). Paper 1 can pre-register a concave dose–response and test whether LLM planners show it.
- **Provenance labels:** humans make only partial use of explicit same-source information.
  - Yousif et al.: none at all, even right after agreeing that independence matters.
  - Connor Desai et al.: partial, when the route from evidence to conclusion is explained.
  - Mercier & Miton: yes, for some concrete cues.

  This makes the labels × copies interaction a well-motivated test for LLMs, with no obvious expected outcome.
- In humans, the chain is complete only if separate findings are combined: repetition raises confidence (Weaver, Yousif, Fazio), and lower confidence increases information seeking (Desender). "Repetition → less verification" itself appears untested in humans in what I found. This is the clearest novelty opening for Paper 1, with the caveat from Auffray (2024) in §1.

### Gaps
- Not checked: the Dechêne et al. (2010) meta-analysis, Pennycook, Cannon & Rand (2018), Unkelbach et al. (2019), Henderson et al.'s (2022) systematic map, and Kelley & Lindsay (1993) on prior exposure and confidence.
- Harkins & Petty (1987), "Information Utility and the Multiple Source Effect," *JPSP* 52: 260–268: the citation is confirmed, but its findings on perceived source independence are unverified (secondary only: https://poly.land/2019/09/29/the-multiple-source-effect/).
- I found no study that measures fact-checking, lateral reading or information search as a behavioral outcome of repeated exposure.
- A 2025 *PSPB* reanalysis that argues knowledge can take precedence over fluency (challenging Fazio et al.'s account) was seen only through a proxy mirror; unverified.

## 5. Overlap of each work with Paper 1's claim (High / Medium / Low) and the key difference

### Takeaway
No work I found tests the full chain in one design: same-source copies → overconfidence → less verification across verification costs → errors that persist and spread. Each link is established on its own in humans or in theory, and the two closest human studies (Yousif et al. 2019; Weaver et al. 2007) stop at confidence. Paper 1's defensible novelty is:
- the duplication is generated inside one agent's own memory by the system itself;
- copies and provenance are manipulated causally while perceptual input and context length are held fixed;
- verification behavior is measured across a cost sweep;
- the effect is traced downstream into progress judgments and actions.

The L1 link ("duplication → overconfidence") should be framed as a known human and economic phenomenon that is now being tested in LLM agents.

### Cited Findings
- Closest human manipulations of copies from one source:
  - Yousif et al. (2019), true vs. false consensus with equal confidence — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797619856844)
  - Weaver et al. (2007), one repetitive voice judged almost like a chorus — [USC PDF](https://dornsife.usc.edu/norbert-schwarz/wp-content/uploads/sites/231/2023/11/07_jpsp_weaver_et_al_chorus.pdf)
  - Enke & Zimmermann (2019), overlapping signals double-counted — [Enke PDF](https://benjamin-enke.com/pdf/Correlation_neglect.pdf)
- The only direct test of acquisition under correlation neglect I found is Auffray (2024): neglecters pay more for correlated signals and are more overprecise — [WashU](https://repository.library.washu.edu/art_sci_etds/3003).
- Rational theory says agents free-ride on public beliefs and stop acquiring once those beliefs are strong enough — [JEL survey](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.11044); [Ali 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jetheo/v175y2018icp713-729.html).
- Lower confidence with accuracy held fixed leads to more information seeking — [Desender et al. 2018](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10076911/).
- Errors persist through wrong mental models and the confidence they produce, despite feedback — [Esponda, Vespa & Yuksel 2024](https://econ.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2023/05/EspondaVespaYuksel.pdf) — and through actions that are never explored — [Fudenberg & Levine 1993](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1993/05/01/self-confirming-equilibrium).
- Human counter-evidence: people track sources in the field ([Mobius et al.](https://cepr.org/publications/DP10493)), overweight their own signal rather than neglect redundancy in a transparent lab sequence ([Angrisani et al.](https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-03325779)), buy too many costly signals in cascade games ([Kübler & Weizsäcker](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v71y2004i2p425-441.html)), and discount majorities when dependence cues are concrete ([Mercier & Miton](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/utilizing-simple-cues-informational-dependency)).

**Where Paper 1's novelty lies:**
- **Source of the correlation:** the system copies one agent's own perception into its own memory stores; the copies do not come from other people.
- **Design:** copies and provenance are manipulated causally with images and context length held fixed. Human lab studies also hold stimuli fixed, so "causal intervention" alone is not new.
- **Outcome:** verification behavior across a cost sweep, which almost no correlation-neglect study measures.
- **Downstream effects:** propagation into progress judgments and next actions.
- **Subjects:** LLM planners rather than humans.

**Main risks to the claim:** Auffray (2024) and Kübler–Weizsäcker (2004) show that humans may acquire *more* information, not less. Paper 1 must distinguish "consulting memory again or acquiring more copies" from "independent re-observation," and report the sign of each honestly.

**Suggested framing (my wording):** correlation neglect and the illusion of consensus are well documented in humans. Paper 1 asks whether an LLM agent's shared memory manufactures a false consensus out of a single perception, and whether that false consensus suppresses costly verification.

### Gaps
- LLM-specific work (repetition or position bias in LLMs, sycophancy, multi-agent echo chambers, memory provenance) is outside A2's scope and is not covered here.
- Ratings rest mostly on abstracts. Full-text reading could change several of them, especially Auffray, Enke–Zimmermann and Esponda–Vespa–Yuksel.
- I found no 2025–2026 economics or psychology paper that combines duplication, confidence and costly verification. Coverage of the most recent working papers is thin because only targeted searches were run.
